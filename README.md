📐 MathLab — Interactive Mathematics Laboratory

MathLab is an interactive, visual, and experimental mathematics platform designed to make mathematics easier to explore, understand, and appreciate.

Created as a Senior Category Science Exhibition project, MathLab combines mathematics, interactive simulations, visualizations, games, experiments, 3D models, and real-world applications into a single digital laboratory.

Instead of simply presenting formulas and asking students to memorize them, MathLab focuses on a different question:

Why does the mathematics work?

From exploring probability and coordinate geometry to manipulating 3D solids, experimenting with mathematical formulas, and discovering the mathematics hidden inside games and puzzles, MathLab turns abstract mathematical ideas into interactive experiences.

🎯 Project Vision

MathLab is built around four principles:

SEE IT → PLAY WITH IT → EXPERIMENT WITH IT → UNDERSTAND IT

Mathematics is often taught as a collection of formulas, procedures, and rules. MathLab attempts to reveal the reasoning behind those formulas and demonstrate how mathematics appears in games, technology, science, engineering, architecture, and everyday life.

For each applicable solid, MathLab can demonstrate:

The goal isn't simply to provide formulas.

MathLab attempts to answer:

"Where did this formula come from?"

🥫 Example — Why Is the CSA of a Cylinder 2πrh?

A rectangular sheet of paper can be rolled into a cylindrical shape.

When the cylinder is unrolled, its curved surface becomes a rectangle.

Its:

Length = circumference of the circular base = 2πr

Width = height of the cylinder = h

Therefore:

Area = Length × Width

Area = 2πr × h

Therefore:

CSA = 2πrh

This provides a physical and visual explanation of the formula instead of treating it as something that must simply be memorized.

🔺 Example — Why Is a Cone's Volume 1/3πr²h?

MathLab demonstrates the classic cone-and-cylinder volume experiment.

Take:

A hollow cone
A hollow cylinder
Equal base radius r
Equal height h

Fill the cone with water or sand and transfer it into the cylinder.

It takes approximately three full cones to fill the cylinder.

Since:

V(cylinder) = πr²h

and three identical cones have the same capacity:

3V(cone) = πr²h

Therefore:

V(cone) = 1/3πr²h

This turns a seemingly arbitrary fraction into a visual mathematical relationship.

🧩 Combination Solids

MathLab also explores objects constructed from multiple solids.

Examples include:

🎪 Circus tents
💊 Capsules
🌀 Spinning tops
🧱 Blocks with cavities
🔻 Conical structures
🥣 Objects containing scooped-out sections

A major concept demonstrated here is:

Volume is additive or subtractive.

When solids are joined:

V(total) = V₁ + V₂ + ...

When material is removed:

V(total) = V(original) − V(removed)

However, surface area requires more careful reasoning because internal touching surfaces are no longer exposed.

🔢 Magic Squares

Magic Squares demonstrate:

Number patterns
Arithmetic
Symmetry
Combinatorics
Constraints
Permutations

For the standard 3×3 magic square, the numbers 1–9 must be arranged so that every row, column, and main diagonal has the same sum.

The magic constant is:

15

because:

1 + 2 + ... + 9 = 45

and:

45 ÷ 3 = 15

For two six-sided dice, there are 36 equally likely ordered outcomes.

A total of 7 can occur in six ways:

P(7) = 6/36 = 1/6

while a total of 2 has only one:

P(2) = 1/36

This demonstrates why probability can influence strategy.
